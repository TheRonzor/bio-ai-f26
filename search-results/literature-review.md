# Application of AI in Biology: executive summary

**Search cutoff:** October 14, 2026. **Evidence base:** 20 original, peer-reviewed articles first published online in 2025 or 2026. This cross-field selection covers protein engineering, genome biology, cellular analysis and ecology. The synthesis uses the published abstracts; it reports the authors’ findings without independent reanalysis. Paper metadata, access levels and selection decisions appear in [the CSV](literature-review.csv) and [search log](search-log.txt).

## What the studies show

**Evidence — making and modifying proteins.** Several papers put AI proposals through laboratory tests. [Pacesa and colleagues’ BindCraft](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09429-6) designed binders for diverse targets, including receptors, allergens, a toxin and Cas9; the reported experimental success rate ranged from 10% to 100% across targets. [Sun and colleagues’ μProtein](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-025-01103-w) coupled mutation-effect prediction to reinforcement learning and found high-function, multiply mutated β-lactamase variants after training on single-mutation data. [Gelman and colleagues’ METL](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-025-02776-2) used biophysical simulations plus experimental data to predict protein properties and design functional fluorescent-protein variants from 64 examples. These studies agree that learned representations can narrow a huge search space, but each measures success on a different protein task; their headline results are therefore not interchangeable.

**Evidence — extending beyond sequence design.** [Meyer and colleagues’ MEMPLEX](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-59471-1) learned from synthesis experiments to select chemical, lipid and chaperone conditions for cell-free membrane-protein production. It tested more than 20,000 environments across 28 proteins and identified conditions for difficult targets. [Jin and colleagues’ AMPGen](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-025-08282-7) generated antimicrobial-peptide candidates using evolutionary information and diffusion modeling, then synthesized and assayed them against *Escherichia coli* and *Staphylococcus aureus*. These applications differ in their output—a reaction environment versus a new peptide—but both use biological experiments to assess AI-guided choices. **Inference:** the value of a model here depends on how its proposed solution performs in an assay, not merely on a plausible sequence or predicted structure.

**Evidence — editing and interpreting genomes.** [Ruffolo and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09298-z) used a biological language model to generate a functional CRISPR editor and demonstrated human-genome editing. [Silverstein and colleagues’ PAMmla](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09021-y) learned Cas9 target-recognition preferences from engineered variants and used the model to identify tailored editors, with tests of activity and off-target effects. [Naert and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41587-025-02771-0) predicted DNA-repair outcomes to design integration templates that promoted frame-retaining insertion and reduced deletions. Together, they show three distinct points where AI can intervene: generating an editor, selecting its specificity, and anticipating the cell’s repair response. None of those results alone establishes reliable editing at every target or in every cell type.

**Evidence — reading regulatory DNA.** [Avsec and colleagues’ AlphaGenome](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-10014-0) predicts thousands of functional genomic measurements from long human and mouse DNA inputs and benchmarks effects of regulatory variants. [Barbadilla-Martínez and colleagues’ PARM](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-10093-z) instead trained on massively parallel reporter assays of human promoters to connect sequence patterns with promoter activity and transcription-factor effects. Both seek sequence-to-function relationships, but their data strategies differ: broad existing genomic measurements versus targeted functional assays. **Inference:** a model that covers many molecular outputs and a model trained on direct perturbation-like measurements offer complementary evidence about regulation; neither abstract demonstrates that all predicted variant effects are causal in living tissues.

**Evidence — locating and following cells.** [Tejada-Lapuerta and colleagues’ Nicheformer](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-025-02814-z) combined dissociated and spatial single-cell measurements; its comparisons found that models trained only on dissociated cells missed aspects of tissue microenvironments. [Chitra and colleagues’ GASTON](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-024-02503-3) extracted interpretable spatial gene-expression gradients in brain and tumor tissues. [Yang and colleagues’ STAIG](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-56276-0) joined gene expression, location and tissue images to identify spatial regions. These papers agree that preserving spatial context changes what can be inferred from cells. Their reported improvements address different tasks, however, so they do not establish a single best architecture for spatial biology.

**Evidence — reconstructing change over time.** [Huizing and colleagues’ STORIES](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-025-02855-4) inferred spatially informed cell-fate trajectories from developmental and regeneration atlases, recovering known marker trends and suggesting additional drivers. [Vinyard and colleagues’ scDiffEq](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-025-01150-3) modeled deterministic and stochastic cell dynamics and tested fate prediction with lineage-traced haematopoietic cells and simulated perturbations. Both address the difficulty of learning dynamics from cell measurements, but a reconstructed trajectory is a model-based explanation rather than a direct observation of every transition. **Inference:** prospective lineage or perturbation experiments remain important checks on predicted cell fates.

**Evidence — observing ecological systems.** [Ovaskainen and colleagues’ bird digital twin](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-025-02966-3) combined machine-classified audio with continuously updated citizen-science data; independent tests found better bird-distribution predictions. [Andersen and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-64175-7) used graph neural networks to forecast bacterial-community abundance in 24 wastewater-treatment plants and tested transfer to a gut-microbiome dataset. [Ovaskainen and colleagues’ CORAL](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-025-02823-y) improved inference for rare species by transferring information from common ones in a survey containing more than 255,000 Malagasy arthropod species. These studies agree that large, repeated observations enable predictions at scales difficult to analyze manually. They address different ecological questions—location, temporal abundance and rare-species occurrence—so apparent performance gains cannot be ranked against one another.

**Evidence — identifying species.** [Kösters and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1093/sysbio/syaf026) combined DNA and images for species identification. Fusion improved accuracy in three of four tested plant and animal datasets, making the fourth a useful reminder that adding a modality is not automatically beneficial. [Magaldi and colleagues’ DeepForestVision](https://doi.org/10.1002/2688-8319.70167) trained on African tropical-forest camera-trap media and reported 87.7% accuracy on a 23-taxon video test set, outperforming three comparison tools there. The two studies agree that context-specific data and evaluation matter; they do not test the same species, sensors or settings. **Inference:** performance on a benchmark should not be treated as a guarantee for rare species or a new habitat.

## Agreement, tension and open questions

Across these papers, the strongest common claim is practical: AI can turn large biological datasets into candidates, classifications or forecasts that can be tested against experiments or held-out observations. The studies do **not** provide a common measure of “AI success.” For example, METL notes that evolutionary-sequence models remain strong on many assays, while BindCraft reports target-dependent design success; the species-fusion study finds a benefit in most, not all, datasets. These are limits on generalization rather than direct contradictions. The evidence also ranges from wet-lab validation to retrospective benchmarks, and this abstract-based review cannot compare all study designs at full-method depth.

The next questions are whether models retain accuracy when moved to new proteins, cell types, taxa and habitats; how much new experimental data is needed to correct failures; and how to distinguish a mechanistic biological explanation from a useful prediction. Cross-study benchmarks with explicit uncertainty, independent prospective tests, and careful attention to rare or poorly sampled cases would make the next generation of applications easier to compare and trust. Those are **inferences from the limits reported across the selected studies**, not outcomes already established by them.
